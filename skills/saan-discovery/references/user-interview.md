# User interview methods inside Discovery

Use this reference to prepare questions, probe notes, or plan a follow-up. The user normally interviews the customer. Do not make the user act as a fictional customer to fill information they do not possess.

## Choose the respondent and the decision

Establish what the user needs to learn and who can know it. For ToC, identify the person in the situation, their actual role in use or purchase, and the relevant behavioral differences between participants. For ToB/G, an operator can describe the last case; a policy owner can explain authority; an administrator can demonstrate configuration; a data owner can verify provenance. Do not assume one participant knows all of these.

A role-specific guide should identify the purpose, relevant context, a short ordered set of questions, and records worth bringing. Only ask the user for recipient/context information still missing. Put questions requiring other roles in a separate follow-up queue.

## Build a story before testing a solution

1. **Recent case:** ask for one actual instance of the task and reconstruct it in order. If it has already been described in the notes, reuse it and ask only about the missing part.
2. **Locate the friction:** identify the exact step, handoff, wait, correction, or uncertainty.
3. **Understand its cause:** ask what prevented progress and what the person did next. A symptom does not establish the cause.
4. **Understand significance:** establish frequency, affected work, workarounds, and consequences using records where available. Separate estimates from measured numbers and elapsed time from active effort.
5. **Check important variants:** probe the conditions suggested by the case or product dependency, then restate the discovered rule for confirmation.

These are directions, not five mandatory questions. Each prompt should have one main idea. Give a follow-up its own line rather than packing a checklist into one question.

Do not presuppose an outcome that the source has not established. If closure or payment is unknown, ask how the case reached its current state; ask about completion evidence only if the respondent says it completed. A workshop's goal to examine a case through closure does not prove that case has closed.

| Weak prompt | More useful prompt | What to do next |
|---|---|---|
| Would automatic reminders solve your problem? | Where did the last request wait longest? | Ask what prevented that step from proceeding |
| Do you need flexible approvals? | What happened the last time the usual approver was unavailable? | Verify the actual authority and record |
| Would you use this feature? | How did you complete this task most recently? | Later test a concrete proposed workflow separately |
| Is everything in OA? | Which actions for that case were recorded in OA? | Probe the remaining actions and records without assuming integration |
| Why didn't staff follow the procedure? | What happened when the required information was unavailable? | Distinguish policy from the actual workaround |
| Should the threshold be 50,000? | What source defines the amount boundary? | Check its exact comparison, currency, amount basis, and scope individually |

## Keep facts separate from preferences

Do not prefill factual questions with a recommended customer answer. Avoid wording where agreeing with the interviewer implies a rule has been confirmed.

It is reasonable to recommend an interview order or verification method to the user. Explain why the choice matters. For customer goals, ask for the desired result and constraints rather than claiming a target on their behalf.

When introducing the product, make the transition clear: current-practice discovery has ended and solution-fit testing has begun. Record demonstration feedback separately. Ask the customer to walk a real case through the proposed approach and identify what would fail or change; enthusiasm alone is not acceptance.

## Adapt to consumer discovery

- Start with a recent actual attempt, including what triggered it and what alternative the person used. Do not assume they already use, pay for, or return to the product.
- Ask about motivation or feelings in relation to that episode; do not invent a persona or psychological cause from demographic labels.
- Keep reported preference, observed behavior, and demonstrated product use separate. Interest in a feature does not establish adoption, willingness to pay, retention, or causation.
- Compare meaningful contexts and behaviors across participants. A few interviews can expose a problem or difference; they do not establish population prevalence or a conversion rate.
- Use funnel, purchase, cancellation, or repeat-use measures only when relevant and supported by appropriately scoped evidence. Do not require retention metrics for every consumer task.

## Adapt to enterprise and public-service constraints

- No workaround does not prove low pain. Staff may lack authority, budget, access, or an alternative process.
- Do not infer priority from willingness to pay when the operator is not the buyer or decision-maker.
- Do not require a fixed number of interviews to recognize a rule. Track which relevant roles, organizations, and important variants have been covered. One outlier may reveal a critical exception.
- Do not infer another person's motives. Phrase organizational friction as observed handoffs, missing authority, incompatible goals, or reported concerns, with sources.
- A manager's policy statement and an operator's account may both be accurate for different contexts. Retain both until their scopes are understood.
- For public-facing services, investigate the recipient's experience as well as the delivery organization's responsibility. A public body's acceptance does not establish that residents can complete the service journey.

## Handle unknowns and time limits

For an unknown, record the precise question, blocked decision, suitable role or unassigned owner, useful evidence, and any agreed next action. Unknown deadlines stay unspecified.

At a time limit, prioritize a coherent core path and consequential exceptions. Move unrelated or dependent probes to follow-up. Return a usable interim artifact; do not add a final round of questions solely to declare completion.

For continuation, read the previous brief, identify new evidence, mark which question IDs it resolves and to what extent, and ask only residual questions. New answers can reopen a downstream fit judgment; they need not reopen every earlier question.
